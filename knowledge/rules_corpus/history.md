# History of Chaturanga

Chaturanga is one of the earliest known ancestors of chess, played in India
from around the 6th century CE onward. The name means "four divisions" or
"four limbs" (chatur = four, anga = limb/division), referring to the four
divisions of an ancient Indian army: infantry, cavalry, elephantry, and
chariotry. These four divisions became the Padati (foot soldier), Ashva
(horse), Gaja (elephant), and Ratha (chariot) pieces.

Chaturanga is believed to have spread westward into Persia, where it became
known as Shatranj. From Shatranj, the game spread into the Islamic world
and eventually into medieval Europe, where over several centuries its
pieces and rules evolved into modern chess. The Mantri (counselor/minister)
piece, which could only move one square diagonally, is the direct ancestor
of the modern Queen -- one of the most dramatic transformations in the
game's history, since the Queen became the most powerful piece on the
board despite descending from one of the weakest.

There is significant historical debate about the exact original rules of
Chaturanga, since surviving textual descriptions are incomplete and
sometimes contradictory. Some historical variants were played on the same
8x8 board used for modern chess (Ashtapada), while earlier or regional
variants may have used different board sizes or additional rules (such as
dice-based piece movement in some early forms, which most modern
reconstructions -- including this implementation -- do not include).

Unlike modern chess, most historical reconstructions of Chaturanga agree
that there was no castling, no en passant capture, and no two-square
initial pawn advance -- all of these are later additions from the
Shatranj-to-chess evolution in Persia and Europe.
